# The astronomy is not decoration

Part of the [Vault Orrery](../README.md) documentation.

A view like this can use the vocabulary of a sky without owing it anything —
call a thing a corona, draw a soft circle, and nobody checks. The rule here is
the opposite one: if the code uses a word, the word has to be doing work in it.

- **Orbits are ellipses with the star at a focus**, and Kepler's equation is
  solved for every body every frame. So the second law is visible: a planet
  hurries through periapsis and loiters at apoapsis, exactly as far as its
  eccentricity says it should. Periods go as the semi-major axis to the three
  halves, and they follow when a knob moves the orbits.
- **Moons keep one face to their planet**, the way every large moon in the
  Solar System does. Because rotation is uniform and travel is not, the face
  rocks a few degrees each way over an orbit — libration, which is not
  simulated but simply what having a real ellipse means.
- **Rings sit inside the Roche limit** and lie in their planet's equatorial
  plane, because that is the only place a ring can be and the only plane it
  can survive in. The band across them is Saturn's measured profile, Cassini
  Division and Encke gap included, and the planet's shadow falls across it.
- **A moon eclipsed by its planet goes copper, not black**, because the only
  light that reaches it has been through that planet's air — with a soft
  shadow edge, because that is the part of the shadow from which some of the
  star is still visible.
- **A system is flat and its moons lie in their planet's equator**, the way a
  thing condensed from a disc and held by an equatorial bulge has to be. Turn
  ORBIT SHELL up and it opens out into a sphere anyway; that is a knob and
  not a claim.
- **A world's limb is Rayleigh and Mie scattering**, not a blue outline: it
  goes blue at noon and copper at the terminator because that is what a long
  path through air does to light, and a world with its star behind it wears a
  white halo because particles that size scatter forwards.
- **Every star has all of it, not just the hub.** The folder stars were a
  flat-coloured ball under a wireframe icosahedron with two rings turning over
  it, which is exactly the construction the hub was rescued from and exactly
  what the code calls the tell: a wireframe is how you draw an object you are
  explaining rather than one you are looking at. One of those was fixed and
  the ten to forty in front of it were not. They compile the same photosphere
  now, and which star is being drawn is a define rather than a design.
- **A star's temperature comes from its mass, and so does everything else.**
  Above about half a solar mass the main sequence is one parameter: colour,
  size and life all follow from mass and nothing else. A folder's star is
  sized by how many notes are in it, so the same number sets the temperature,
  and the busiest folder in a vault is its hottest star. Spread over F to M
  and weighted to the cool end, because that is the shape of the real
  distribution — O and B stars are a few hundredths of one per cent of what
  exists, and a wheel of blue giants is not a galaxy.
- **The star has limb darkening, granulation, supergranulation, faculae,
  spots, spicules and prominences**, it turns faster at its equator than at
  its poles on the measured law, and its spot bands walk toward the equator
  over a cycle. Colours come out of Planck's law and the CIE colour matching
  functions rather than off a palette.
- **An ice halo is uneven the way a real one is**, made by whatever crystals
  lie along that line of sight — with the twenty-two and forty-six degree
  rings, sun dogs, tangent arcs above and below, a pillar, the parhelic
  circle, and the circumzenithal arc that opens upward and keeps its violet.
- **Diffraction spikes are beaded and chromatic**, because a spike is the
  Fraunhofer pattern of a straight edge and its fringe spacing goes as the
  wavelength.
- **The corona follows Baumbach's measured brightness law** and is pearl-white
  rather than amber, because that is what Thomson-scattered photospheric
  light is. The glare around a star is the published CIE glare equation, not
  a curve picked to look right.
- **The Milky Way is lumpy, narrows toward the anticentre, and its rift only
  runs down one side**, because the star clouds are sight lines down an arm,
  the band is a disc seen from inside it rather than a ribbon laid over the
  sky, and the Great Rift is one nearby molecular complex rather than a belt.
- **A spiral arm carries a dust lane on its concave side** with the young blue
  associations standing in a line just outside it, which is what the shock at
  the leading edge of a standing wave leaves behind.
- **The comet has two tails**, because comets do: a curved dust tail the
  colour of reflected sunlight, and a straight blue ion tail along the
  anti-solar line where the solar wind has taken the gas.
- **There are three kinds of world, and size is what decides which.** Whether
  a body ends up a giant is whether it gathered enough mass to hold hydrogen
  before the disc's gas was gone — around ten Earth masses, and a hard enough
  line that the Solar System has nothing sitting on it. Below that a body
  keeps only the atmosphere it can make; below that again it keeps none and is
  bare rock. Here a body's radius is its citation count and its length, so the
  vault's most-cited notes come out as the giants. Moons and the archive are
  rock, because in the Solar System they are. The albedos are the measured
  ones — Jupiter 0.52, Earth 0.31, the Moon 0.12 — and that spread is most of
  why the three read as different substances rather than three palettes.
- **A giant's bands are its own rotation.** Coriolis turns convection into
  east-west jets, and what you see is the alternation between them: zones,
  where gas rises and the ammonia deck sits high and bright, and belts, where
  it sinks and you see deeper, warmer and darker. The jets meander rather than
  ruling straight lines, which is also what puts the curls on the boundary
  between a belt and the zone beside it — exactly where the shear is. The
  bands are widest at the equator and crowd toward the poles, the poles wear a
  haze hood rather than an ice cap, and a long-lived oval sits between two
  opposed jets on the bodies whose seed asks for one.
- **A giant's aurorae are hydrogen, not oxygen.** Everyone's picture of an
  aurora is the 557.7 nm oxygen line, which is green and needs an atmosphere
  with oxygen in it. A hydrogen giant's is H₂ band emission and Hα, which
  photographs blue-violet with a red fringe. It is the one thing on a world
  visible on the night side, because it is not reflected light.
- **A crater field is a power law, and it has relief.** The size-frequency
  distribution of impact craters is the most-measured thing about any airless
  surface — many small, few large — so the radius drawn for each cell is a
  power of a uniform rather than a uniform, and the nearest crater is the
  nearest *in units of its own radius*, which is what puts the big ones on top
  of the small ones. The profile is a real one: a floor below the plain, a
  wall, a rim raised above it. Drawn as a bend in the surface normal rather
  than as a change of colour, because a crater seen only as a stain is not
  what anybody recognises — the shading on a bowl is.
- **A giant is visibly flattened, about its own spin axis.** Jupiter's polar
  radius is 93% of its equatorial one and Saturn's is 90% — visible in a pair
  of binoculars, and the first thing anybody notices drawing one. A rocky body
  is held in shape by its own strength instead (Earth's flattening is 1/298,
  under half a pixel here), so only the giants carry it. It goes about the
  spin axis rather than about world up, which is what makes an axial tilt read
  as a tilt, and it puts the world in the same plane its rings and moons are
  already in.
- **A world's ocean glints.** Water is the one genuinely mirror-like surface a
  world has, and the specular patch it throws back is the brightest thing in
  any photograph of an ocean from orbit. Only where there is open sea to catch
  it — not under cloud, not on ice, not on land — and in the star's own
  colour, because a mirror has no colour of its own.
- **A rock brightens sharply at full phase.** The full Moon is about twice as
  bright as twice the half Moon, which is not what any smooth-sphere shading
  gives. Shadow hiding — at zero phase angle every shadow is behind the thing
  that casts it — and coherent backscatter, both of which need the surface to
  be a jumble rather than a surface. It is a few degrees wide, and it is why a
  full Moon reads as a flat disc rather than a lit ball. On rock only: neither
  a cloud deck nor an ocean is a pile of rubble.
- **A world's coastlines are fractal at every scale**, because they are one
  threshold on a fractal height field, and the ice line follows the cold
  rather than a parallel: it takes in the poles and the high ground, and it
  wanders, because what it follows is a temperature contour over terrain.
- **The zodiacal light lies in the plane and has a gegenschein.** The dust
  density falls as r^-1.3 (Leinert et al. 1998, the fan model the Helios and
  Pioneer photometry settled on), which a thin sheet seen from outside
  integrates to a surface brightness near r^-2.3; the grains are micron-sized
  and scatter forward, so the sheet is brightest looked at toward the star,
  with a Henyey–Greenstein lobe at g = 0.55; and it has the narrow
  backscatter peak opposite the star from wherever you stand, a few degrees
  wide, which is the counterglow. Slightly redder than the star, because
  every measurement of the real one is.
- **A planet's halo obeys the inverse square.** The same body on the same
  ellipse is lit harder at periapsis than at apoapsis by the square of the
  ratio of the two distances — at the eccentricities here up to a factor of
  two, which is Mars at a perihelic opposition against an aphelic one. On
  top of the phase it already carried: full with its star behind you, a
  sliver when it stands between you and the star.
- **The orbital elements are on the inspector**, because a claim you cannot
  check is decoration. Semi-major axis as drawn this frame, eccentricity,
  the period at the current ORBIT SPEED in wall-clock time, and the
  inclination of the plane the body is actually on — the four numbers
  Kepler's equation is being solved with for the body you selected, read
  back out.

What is deliberately not to scale is said out loud where it happens, and
there are two kinds of it. Clocks are compressed: a star that turned once in
twenty-seven days would never appear to turn at all, and a spot cycle at its
true length would be a constant. And bodies are drawn far larger against
their orbits than they are, because a note you cannot see is not a note —
which is why satellite systems here are wider relative to their planets than
a Hill sphere would allow, and could not be otherwise while a planet is
several pixels across from three rings away.

Neither of those is licence to invent the shape of a thing, and the shapes
are what this list is about.
